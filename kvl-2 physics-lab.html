Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) Explained Loop Rule

Fundamental Physics, The Conservation of Energy, and How to Apply It

1. What is Kirchhoff's Voltage Law?

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (also known as Kirchhoff's Second Law or the Loop Rule) states that:

"The algebraic sum of all electric potential differences (voltages) around any closed loop in a circuit is strictly equal to zero."
Σ Vloop = 0

Stated simply: Total Potential Rises (from batteries/sources) = Total Potential Drops (across resistors/loads).

2. Why does it work? (Conservation of Energy)

KVL is not an arbitrary rule; it is a direct consequence of the Law of Conservation of Energy.

  • In electrostatic conditions, the electric field Ê is conservative: ∮ E • dl = 0.
  • Electric potential V represents electric potential energy per unit charge (V = U / q).
  • When a charge q is transported around a complete closed loop and arrives back at its exact starting position, its net change in potential energy must be zero (ΔU = 0 ⇒ ΔV = 0).
  • The chemical energy supplied by the battery (Pin = Vs × I) is exactly equal to the thermal and work energy dissipated by the resistors (Pout = I2R1 + I2R2 + I2R3).

3. Real-World Analogy: The Hiking Mountain Loop

Imagine hiking on a closed mountain trail:

🚠 The Battery (Ski-Lift) Lifts you from ground level (0m) up to the mountain peak (+12 meters). This is a Potential Rise.
⛷️ The Resistors (Ski Slopes) Slope 1 drops you by -5m, slope 2 drops you by -4m, slope 3 drops you by -3m.

When you return to the base lodge where you started: +12m - 5m - 4m - 3m = 0 meters net elevation change! You are back at reference ground level!

4. Golden Sign Convention Rules

When writing a KVL equation around a loop in a chosen traversal direction (Clockwise or Counter-Clockwise):

Across Voltage Sources (Battery):
− to + (low to high): +V (Rise)
+ to − (high to low): −V (Drop)
Across Resistors (Current I):
With current direction: −I•R (Drop)
Against current direction: +I•R (Rise)

5. Key Mathematical Formulas

Total Series R Rtot = Σ Ri
Loop Current I = Vs / Rtot
Voltage Divider Vk = Vs × (Rk / Rtot)

When the loop closes at the starting point, exactly 0V remains! ΣV = 0.
